From the Editor Issue 47 · April 2026
A note by The Art Direction, set in the margin, where notes belong.

This is Magazine. We believe a patient encounter is a story worth designing — not a row in a database. We refuse the visual flatness of CRUD apps and the false equality of grid-based dashboards. A clinic is a place of attention, of breath caught in a stairwell, of a voice trailing off mid-sentence; we lay it out the way a printer lays out a feature — with hierarchy, with margins, with pull-quotes, with the page-number running quietly at the foot.

We want a chart that reads like a feature article. We want a dashboard that reads like a contents page. We want every visit to feel like it has been edited, not merely filed. Welcome to issue forty-seven.

— The Editorial Desk, set in italic 12 pt Cormorant.

Photo Essay · A12 · The Quiet Hour

The hour between visits

We don’t document the hour between visits, but the scribe does. It listens for the squeak of a chair in the next room, the long exhale of a clinician between charts, the second drawer of the refrigerator opening for a lukewarm coffee. The hour between visits is when the day is composed into something a doctor can sign with a clear hand. It is the hour we set aside for the magazine to do its work: lay out the next page, choose the pull-quote, set the page numbers in the lower margin, and quietly pass the issue back to the desk.

The visit just past becomes a feature; the visit just ahead becomes a deadline. The scribe waits with the photographer in the lobby, both of them watching the door.

Glossary · A13 · for readers new to the issue

A short lexicon, set in four columns.

SOAP. The four-part structure of a clinical note — Subjective, Objective, Assessment, Plan. The scribe drafts each part as a separate column, then composites them into a single page. We treat it as a quartet.

Layer. One of seven processing stages between the raw audio and a signed note. Layers are numbered 01—07, with 07 split into a, b, c, d, e for coding, prescribing, referral and patient summary.

Confidence. A 0—1 score the scribe assigns to itself, after self-critique in Layer 05. Anything below 0.80 we flag in italic in the margin and ask a human to read.

Specialty. The lens through which the scribe listens — cardiology, endocrinology, orthopaedics. The same audio yields different notes through different lenses. We let the doctor choose the lens.

Diarisation. The act of telling the patient’s voice apart from the doctor’s. The scribe uses speaker turns (SPK 0, SPK 1) and lets the editor reassign with a click if the diariser misheard.

Pull-quote. A line from the patient (or sometimes the doctor) that the scribe sets in 32 pt italic to mark something worth remembering. It is, in our editorial opinion, the heart of the visit.

Marginalia. A note set in italic in the gutter, beside the running text. We use it for clinical context the doctor might want a beat to read, but not interrupt the visit for.

Errata. A formal correction. We collect them on Page A9 each issue, set in italic with leading numerals.

Folio. The page number, set in italic in the margin. We never set it in bold; we feel that’s undignified.

Kicker. The short tagline above a headline, set in 10.5 pt mono with letterspacing. We use it the way an editor uses a tag.

Drop cap. The first letter of a major body block, enlarged. We set it in burnt orange. Apartamento sets it in mossy green. We deferred to our own colour.

Spread. Two facing pages designed as one. The recording on Page A2—A3 is a spread. The dashboard, arguably, is a four-page spread.

Long Read · A16 · an essay on the verb to listen

The verb to listen,
in twelve tenses.

On the small, often-skipped grammar of paying attention — what changes when a clinic stops typing and starts hearing.

A patient says just fourteen times in eleven minutes, and a doctor — if she is the kind we like to be — hears it as a softening. The word just appears in the margin of half a sentence; it is what people say when they are trying to make their pain smaller for the listener. We have known this for centuries; it is in Aristotle, somewhere; it is certainly in nursing school. What is new is that a machine can hear it too.

The scribe does not interrupt; it does not even, strictly speaking, type. It listens. After the visit, it composes — in the way a magazine composes — a feature out of what was said. It is tempting to say the scribe is a stenographer, but a stenographer transcribes; the scribe edits. It chooses the pull-quote. It sets the chest tightness in italic. It puts the home blood-pressure reading in the margin, beside a small graph.

We design QuillMD for the editing, not the typing. The visit is a story. The note is the spread. The doctor is the editor — she signs the page only when she has read it, and we feel strongly that the page should be worth reading. A SOAP note designed in a database table is a SOAP note nobody reads twice. A SOAP note laid out as a feature is one a colleague might pick up and work from.

This issue is the first to commit fully to the page metaphor. The recording is a spread. The dashboard is a contents page. The patient list is a classifieds back-page in mono. The footnotes are marginalia. The numbers are set in eighty-point Bodoni, because numbers in a magazine are themselves a design element. We are not trying to be precious; we are trying to be read.

A machine that can hear is one thing. A machine that can lay it out is another. The two together, we hope, are something a clinic can use without becoming, itself, a database.

On the next page, on Page A17 of next week’s issue, we will publish the order set Dr. Rao signed at 14:42 PST — an EKG today, a stress test within fourteen days, a follow-up call from the front desk on Friday. We will also publish a short piece on why we do not use red, the colour, in this issue. (We use burnt orange. We mixed it once. We have committed.)

Until then: a small thank-you, set in italic, to every patient who has let the scribe listen. Without you, there is no story. There is only a database.
